Who's behind this

One builder, his own money, and a system that publishes its failures.

Rick Winnett builds Handspike from western North Carolina, where he moved to care for his parents. He is a builder and technologist by trade, and Sovereign began as a private answer to a private problem: how to make his own capital work harder without handing it to anyone — or to an unsupervised model.

Every dollar Sovereign has traded is his. There are no outside investors, no clients, and nothing for sale yet.

Why this is built the way it is

Skin in the game, and no one to impress.

A trading system funded by strangers has an incentive to look good. This one has the opposite incentive: it is measured against the founder's own money, so a comfortable lie costs him directly. That is why the rails, the watchdogs, and the accounting exist — and why the failures get published rather than quietly patched.

  • His capital, his risk. No outside money, no clients, no fiduciary duty anywhere in the picture. The live figure on the front page is his own account, deposit-neutral and unedited — losses and all.
  • Nothing hosted, nothing held. When Sovereign does ship, it runs on the customer's own machine with the customer's own keys. Handspike is structurally unable to touch anyone's money, which is the point rather than a limitation.
  • Failures first. The public record leads with eleven production failures because a safety product that only advertises its wins is selling the wrong thing.

What he does for a living today

Sovereign is not the business yet — the business is the engineering underneath it. If your autonomous agents take irreversible real-world actions (spend money, place orders, send things to people), the deterministic safety layer that keeps a capable model from doing something expensive is the same work, in a domain that is not securities.
