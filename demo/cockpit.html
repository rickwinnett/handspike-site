Two deliberate actions, never one — the cover exists so that no single mis-tap can arm real capital. The lid falls shut again after every throw, and a lifted cover you do not throw re-locks on its own. On the real cockpit each arm also asks for the PIN again. What you are looking at

  • Orange means real money, and nothing else does. Paper trading is amber, the interactive accent is blue, and red is reserved for loss and danger. An accent in the red family would make "this control is available" and "you are losing money" the same colour, so the palette rules that out at the token level.
  • Arming takes two actions, not one. Lift the cover, then throw the switch. The covers are not theatre: they are there because the failure mode for an autonomous trading system is a single careless tap, and a control that can be armed by accident will eventually be armed by accident.
  • On the real cockpit the line is recorded, never redrawn. There, every point is written down as the book is read; the solid line steps only when money was actually made or lost, and a trade's dot sits on the read that first saw it close. Nothing is smoothed, interpolated or reconstructed after the fact. The series on this page is representative, drawn the same way.
  • Every number carries the sentence that explains it. The prose inside each tile is not a tooltip — it is the reason the figure can be checked rather than trusted. Open one and it tells you how it was derived and what would make it wrong.
  • It withholds rather than guesses. A venue that did not answer prints a dash and its reason, never a fresh-looking zero; a total that is short by an unread account is not shown at all. On a money surface, a number that invents itself is worse than one that admits it is blind.
  • The refusals are the product. Drill-down is not an error log. It is the record of every trade the system chose not to make, with its reason and its grade — including the ones where declining turned out to be wrong.
  • Private network, behind a PIN. The real cockpit reaches its operator on their own tailnet behind a PIN, and arming real money asks for it again. It is not on the public internet, and this page is a rebuild rather than a window into it.

Where the real one lives

The cockpit runs on the owner's own machine, reachable only over the owner's private network. There is no hosted version, no shared instance, and no account of yours for us to hold.
